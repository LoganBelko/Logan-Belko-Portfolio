Net zero for a building that's already good

Barrio Metalico is a nine-unit infill project that was efficient from day one. This retrofit strategy asks how to take it the rest of the way in a climate that tops 94°F for nearly four months a year.

The challenge

Designed by Rob Paulus Architects in Tucson's Millville neighborhood, Barrio Metalico already has super-insulated walls and roofs, solar-conscious orientation, salvaged materials, rainwater harvesting, and plumbing ready for solar hot water. Some units already carry rooftop panels.

That makes it a harder and more realistic question than a typical retrofit. There's no obvious flaw to fix. The work is in targeted refinements that cut what's left of the cooling load, then sizing renewables to cover the rest.

The approach

Following the Path to Net Zero framework, every step reduces demand before the next one adds supply. A smaller load means a smaller array, which means a cheaper, more realistic path to net zero.

  1. BaselineDocument what already works: insulation, orientation, compact massing, water harvesting.
  2. PassiveBlock solar heat before it gets inside: shading, glazing, cool roofs, airflow.
  3. ActiveCool what's left efficiently: heat pumps, zoning, smart controls, solar hot water.
  4. Net zeroSize rooftop solar to offset the remaining, now smaller, demand.
Step 02 · Passive

Stop the heat at the facade

Several units take direct western sun through large windows in the late afternoon, exactly when outdoor temperatures and cooling demand peak.

  1. Exterior shadingVertical fins, perforated metal screens, and shallow overhangs on the west and south faces.
  2. Low-e glazingCuts solar heat gain on the most exposed windows while keeping daylight.
  3. Cool roofs and plantingReflective roof coatings and added vegetation to reduce absorbed and reflected heat.
  4. Stack ventilationThe sloped roofs and narrow floor plates already let warm air rise and exit; the strategy builds on that.
Step 03 · Active

Cool what's left, efficiently

Cooling is the dominant load in southern Arizona for most of the year, so even modest gains in HVAC efficiency show up across the whole site.

  1. Heat pump mini-splitsHigh-efficiency, all-electric cooling sized for compact, well-insulated units.
  2. Zoned cooling and smart controlsCondition occupied rooms only, and ease off during low occupancy and cooler nights.
  3. Solar hot waterUse the existing pre-plumbed infrastructure, no structural changes needed.
  4. LED lighting and efficient appliancesLower electricity use and less internal heat for the cooling system to remove.
Step 04 · Net zero

Size the solar to the smaller load

Simple roof forms, good sun access, and some of the highest solar exposure in the country make grid-tied rooftop PV the clearest renewable path here. Wind and bioenergy don't fit a dense infill site.

What it shows

The best path to net zero here is refinement, not redesign. Solar on nearly every roof gets the development most of the way. The remaining gap, about 16% against an average household, closes through shading, glazing, and efficient cooling instead of more panels.

The broader lesson applies to every growing desert city: compact infill housing can reach net zero when it reduces heat first and builds renewables second.

Limits and next steps

The passive and active strategies are qualitative. The solar figure is a single PVWatts estimate built on an assumed system size, and the household comparison uses Arizona's statewide average, so these small, super-insulated units likely use less than that. An energy model of the units, plus monitored energy use, would put real numbers on how much each step cuts.
