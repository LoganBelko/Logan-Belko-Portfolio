When low utilization still feels crowded

Spatial demand, LEED context, and user experience in University of Arizona recreation centers

The question

Recreation centers are usually judged by capacity and equipment counts. Those numbers don't explain how a space feels to the people using it.

This study compared SouthREC, the first collegiate recreation center to earn LEED Platinum, with the smaller, three-level NorthREC. It asked whether layout, equipment concentration, and environmental conditions shape user experience more directly than total capacity.

Approach

A mixed-method post-occupancy evaluation, pairing direct observation of equipment use with short intercept surveys at the same sessions.

Sites
SouthREC (LEED Platinum, about 130,000 sq ft) and NorthREC (about 52,000 sq ft, three levels)
Observations
259 counts of cardio machine use, February 23 to March 21, 2026
Surveys
106 responses from 370 users approached, covering crowding, comfort, movement, equipment access, and how people adapted
Schedule
11AM and 5PM sessions, Monday through Saturday, alternating which facility came first each day and week
Analysis
Utilization rates, survey scoring, and charts built in Google Sheets

Findings

Every session fell in the low-utilization range (under 30%). Users still rated crowding as moderate, between 2.4 and 3.0 out of 5, and access problems climbed sharply at 5PM.

Demand piled onto two machine types

MachineNorthREC 11AMSouthREC 11AMNorthREC 5PMSouthREC 5PM

Treadmills and Stairmasters carried most of the load, peaking near 47% at SouthREC in the evening. Arc trainers, hand pedals, and other equipment went unused in every session.

When blocked, people waited instead of moving

Share of respondents who couldn't access equipment, by what they did next. Users stayed in their preferred zone, so even a few busy machines created congestion in one spot.

Layout

SouthREC concentrates cardio on one open floor, along the north edge with the strongest daylight and views. NorthREC splits activity across three levels. In both buildings, demand clustered in a few zones rather than spreading across the available equipment.

Design implications

  • Adding equipment won't fix crowding if demand stays concentrated on a few machine types.
  • Place high-demand machines in accessible, well-circulated locations.
  • Pair environmental features like daylight and views with the equipment people actually use.
  • Evaluate sustainable buildings on how they perform in use, alongside how they're certified.

Limitations and next steps

The study covered two facilities, two daily time blocks, and self-reported survey data. Environmental conditions were discussed qualitatively. Future work could add direct measurements of temperature, airflow, and daylight alongside behavioral data, and extend observation to more times of day.
