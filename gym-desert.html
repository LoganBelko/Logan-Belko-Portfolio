Gym deserts in St. Louis

Where can people actually go to work out? Nearly half of the city and county lives in a ZIP code with fewer than 3 gyms, and those same ZIP codes have the region's highest rates of obesity, diabetes, and physical inactivity.

47%of city and county residents, about 636,000 people, live in ZIP codes with fewer than 3 gyms.

Why this question

Nearly two years as a personal trainer at the University of Arizona's recreation centers shaped how I think about equity. It includes whether a person has a safe, affordable place nearby to move.

"Food deserts" are a familiar idea. This project asks the same question about exercise: where in St. Louis are people farthest from places to be active, and do those places line up with worse health?

Choosing the right data

The obvious free source for gym locations is OpenStreetMap, the volunteer-built world map. Before building on it, I checked it against the Census Bureau's count of fitness businesses in every ZIP code, which comes from tax and payroll records.

In 19 of the 29 ZIP codes the Census reports in detail, OpenStreetMap had fewer than half of the gyms, about 120 missing in all. The gaps were worst in the suburbs. Built on the free data, Chesterfield would have looked like a gym desert, the opposite of the truth.

So the map uses the Census count for private gyms, as gyms per 10,000 residents. The Census leaves out government-run facilities, so I built a separate list of public rec centers and YMCAs from city, county, and municipal sources. That list has 45 facilities; OpenStreetMap had 21, two of which turned out to be a campus gym and a youth sports league.

Outdoor fitness stations aren't: there are five times as many per resident in the gym deserts, mostly in North City and North County.

The gap behaves like a threshold

Among ZIP codes with 3 or more gyms, more gyms didn't go with better health: none of the four measures correlated significantly with the gym rate. The difference is between having almost nothing nearby and having something. Going from 3 gyms per 10,000 residents to 10 adds little.

The deepest deserts aren't the least healthy

The 10 ZIP codes with no gyms, rec centers, or outdoor stations include suburban fringe areas like Wildwood, Pacific, High Ridge, and Valley Park, where residents drive and the median ZIP has about 68 acres of parkland per 1,000 people. The worst health numbers are in the low-gym ZIP codes that do have public options, mostly in North City and North County, where the median ZIP has about 6 acres of parkland per 1,000 people. Distance and parkland matter, but so do income and everything that comes with it.

The market left gaps. Some have no backstop.

Private gyms cluster in the central and west county. Nearly half of St. Louis City and County lives in ZIP codes the market has mostly skipped, and those ZIP codes carry the region's heaviest burden of inactivity and chronic disease.

This doesn't prove that missing gyms cause poor health. Income and race shape both, and the CDC's estimates are partly modeled from local demographics. What the maps do show is where public investment in places to move would reach the people with the least access and the most to gain.

  • Build where the market won'tPrioritize new or expanded public fitness space in North City and North County ZIP codes with high inactivity.
  • Start with what's freeOutdoor fitness stations already cluster in the gym deserts. Expanding them is the fastest, cheapest step.
  • Close the true desertsFlorissant (63031), Spanish Lake, Bridgeton, North City's 63115, and South City's 63109 have no gyms, rec centers, or outdoor stations.
  • Measure distance, not bordersPlan by walking and transit time to the nearest option, since a rec center across a ZIP line still serves its neighbors.

Limits and next steps

  • Counts, not locations. The Census reports gyms per ZIP code, not addresses, and doesn't publish exact counts below 3. ZIP codes with 0, 1, or 2 gyms are treated as one group.
  • A broad category. NAICS 713940 includes yoga studios, climbing gyms, and swim and tennis clubs along with conventional gyms.
  • Residents, not visitors. Downtown's high rates partly reflect gyms that serve daily workers, not just the people who live there.
  • Modeled health data. CDC PLACES estimates are modeled from survey and demographic data, not measured in each ZIP code.
  • Hand-built list. The public rec center list reflects city, county, and YMCA listings as of October 2026.
  • Correlation only. These are associations, not causes.

Next, I'd measure walking and transit time from every neighborhood to its nearest public option, add income and race to separate access from the effects of poverty, and replace the ZIP code counts with exact gym locations from a business database.
