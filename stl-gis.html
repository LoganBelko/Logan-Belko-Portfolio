Shade, pavement, and heat in St. Louis

I set out to show that tree canopy and pavement follow race and income. Across 104 census tracts, they don't. Pavement drives the heat, and the city's racial divide shows up in income instead.

62%of the difference in heat between tracts is explained by pavement alone (R² = 0.62).
~1%of the difference in tree canopy is explained by household income (R² = 0.01, not significant).
$34k / $80kMedian household income in tracts that are 80%+ residents of color, against tracts under 20%.

The question

Trees cool streets, soak up stormwater, and clean the air. Pavement and rooftops do the opposite: they hold heat, shed runoff, and raise summer temperatures block by block. St. Louis is also one of the most segregated cities in the country, so it's a natural place to ask who gets which.

The first version of this project compared maps of 2021 canopy and pavement against race and income across 79 neighborhoods, and concluded they lined up. That was a visual comparison. When I rebuilt it with 2025 data, I tested the claim directly: do tree canopy, impervious surface, and heat follow race and income across St. Louis census tracts?

Study area
City of St. Louis, 104 census tracts
Data
Annual NLCD 2025 tree canopy and fractional impervious surface (30 m); Trust for Public Land Heat Severity Index; ACS 2023 5-year income, race, and Hispanic origin
GIS workflow
Select Layer By Location, Extract by Mask, Zonal Statistics as Table, Join Field, Calculate Field in ArcGIS Pro
Analysis
Pearson and Spearman correlation on tract values (n = 104; 103 for income)

Heat was the layer the original project couldn't add. With it, the physical story is clear and strong: more pavement and less canopy mean a hotter tract.

If canopy followed income, the map would mostly be two colors: dark in the corner for wealthy and green, pale for poor and bare. Instead, all nine colors are spread across the city.

Testing it

Land cover predicts heat. Demographics don't.

Bar length is R², the share of variation one measure explains in the other (full width = 0.70). Labels show Pearson's r. Spearman's rank correlation gave the same results.

The four strong relationships are all physical except one, race and income. Every pairing of land cover with race or income is close to zero. The only significant one runs opposite to the hypothesis: tracts with more residents of color are slightly cooler on average.

Heat runs slightly the other way, because downtown and the central corridor hold both higher incomes and the most pavement.

The hypothesis didn't hold. The divide did.

At the tract level, canopy, pavement, and heat in St. Louis don't track race or income. They track each other. What tracks race is money: the north side is almost entirely residents of color, and its households earn less than half of what households in the whitest tracts do.

That doesn't mean the north side is better off environmentally. Satellite canopy can't tell a maintained street tree from brush growing over a vacant lot, and North St. Louis holds much of the city's vacant land. A tract average also hides the block-by-block differences people actually live with. The data shows where to look next. It doesn't show the problem is solved.

  • Cool by pavementAim heat programs at the most sealed tracts, downtown, the riverfront, and the central corridor, where pavement explains most of the heat.
  • Count the right treesSeparate street and yard canopy from vacant-lot growth before calling a tract well shaded.
  • Go finer than tractsRepeat at the block-group level with surface temperature to catch what averages hide.
  • Keep equity in viewPair greening and depaving with anti-displacement protections, especially north of the divide, where incomes are lowest.

Limits and next steps

  • Tract averages. Each tract holds about 2,800 people. Averaging hides variation within it, and a pattern across tracts doesn't describe any single household.
  • What canopy measures. NLCD canopy is a 30-meter satellite estimate. It counts all tree cover equally, whether it's a shaded street or an overgrown lot.
  • Relative heat. The heat index ranks places against their surroundings. It isn't a measured air or surface temperature.
  • Survey error. ACS figures are estimates with margins of error, which are widest in small tracts.
  • Correlation only. These are associations, not causes.

Next, I'd add the city's vacant-parcel data to separate vacant-lot canopy from street canopy, rerun the analysis at the block-group level, and bring in land surface temperature from Landsat.
