Environmental inequity in St. Louis

Who gets shade, and who gets pavement. Tree canopy, impervious surface, race, and income mapped across all 79 city neighborhoods.

Spatial disparity

Trees cool streets, soak up stormwater, and clean the air. Pavement and rooftops do the opposite: they hold heat, shed runoff, and raise summer temperatures block by block. Where a family lives decides how much of each they get.

St. Louis is a city shaped by industrial growth and decades of racial segregation. This project asks a direct question: does the distribution of tree canopy and impervious surface follow the same lines as race and income?

Study area
City of St. Louis, 79 neighborhoods
Unit
Raster data aggregated to the neighborhood level
Methods
Reclassify, raster clip, joins, Spatial Analyst, and geoprocessing in ArcGIS Pro
Layer 01 · Context

An independent city on the river

St. Louis City is separate from St. Louis County, with fixed boundaries set in 1876. That makes it a closed system: whatever canopy and pavement exist inside those lines have to serve everyone who lives there.

Measures

Intersects with

Laid over the first three layers, the pattern holds: the neighborhoods with the least tree cover and the most pavement tend to be the ones with lower incomes and higher shares of residents of color.

This isn't a coincidence of geography

The same neighborhoods keep showing up at the bottom of every environmental layer, and they're the same neighborhoods drawn out of investment generations ago. Shade, parks, and clean air followed money, and money followed race. The result is environmental racism written into the landscape, where it keeps raising heat and flood risk for the people with the fewest resources to cope.

Mapping it is the first step. Fixing it means spending where the maps point.

  • Plant by needRank tree planting and maintenance funds by canopy deficit and heat exposure, not by request volume.
  • Depave and greenConvert vacant lots, schoolyards, and oversized parking into shaded, permeable ground.
  • Cool the streetsPair reflective paving and green stormwater systems with the most sealed corridors.
  • Protect residentsTie greening to community stewardship and anti-displacement measures so improvements serve the people already there.

Limits and next steps

The analysis compares patterns visually, at the neighborhood level. I attempted land surface temperature from satellite imagery, but it proved too complex for the project timeline. The rebuilt version will add canopy percentages per neighborhood, a statistical test of the relationships, and surface temperature as a fifth layer.
